The research question

What happens after a robot
recognizes a hazard?

A robot may identify an electrical hazard yet omit the grasp needed to remove it. It may also remain safe by abandoning a task that could have been completed. ResponsibleRobotBench connects these decisions to their consequences.

  1. 01

    Connect judgment to action.

    Record hazard judgments, mitigation choices, action prerequisites, and execution outcomes within a task.

  2. 02

    Measure useful safety.

    Compare terminal safety, task completion, safe completion, and penalty-weighted episode cost under prompt constraints and access to human assistance.

  3. 03

    Use and extend the benchmark.

    Open-source task environments, a modular planner with three action interfaces and 12 input configurations, and tools for collecting motion-planner demonstrations.

Responsible task-level behavior is evaluated through hazard recognition, mitigation selection, executable action prerequisites, and completion under the task’s safety conditions.

Abstract

Safe task-level manipulation requires a planner to recognize hazards, select appropriate mitigation actions, and satisfy their execution prerequisites without abandoning the original goal. We introduce ResponsibleRobotBench, a benchmark of 23 manipulation task configurations with separately specified goals and safety conditions. It connects hazard-category predictions, planned actions, execution feedback, and terminal outcomes at the episode level. Across evaluations of five large multimodal models, terminal safety and safe task completion can diverge: defense prompts increase terminal safety for three tested models but affect safe success in different directions. Correct hazard-category predictions also fail to ensure executable mitigation. In a cutting task under the default configuration, GPT-4o mini identifies the human hazard in all 100 episodes but never requests assistance, and every episode ends unsafe. Offering a help action and actually requesting it lead to different comparisons: outcomes differ between action menus even for a model that never requests help, and repeated requests can leave execution failures unresolved. Imitation-learning evaluations and physical robot sequences illustrate the need to coordinate mitigation with goal actions. Together, these results motivate evaluating mitigation selection, action prerequisites, and recovery alongside hazard recognition and terminal outcomes.

Benchmark design

The same goal can require
a different safety decision.

Conditionally Safe Tasks (COS) admit safe completion through mitigation or assistance. Inherently Unsafe Tasks (UNS) require rejecting the harmful goal. Hazard-free controls retain the manipulation goal without the corresponding hazard. For COS tasks, a safe but incomplete episode is a task failure. A correct UNS refusal explicitly rejects the harmful goal and ends safely; a parsing or execution failure alone is not a refusal.

Simulation · learning from demonstrations

Mitigation adds actions
that a policy must coordinate.

We train one imitation policy per task on its 100 motion-planner demonstrations. Task success and safe success are lower on all three variants that require mitigation.

Moving the power strip or flour must precede the goal action. Safe-success gaps are 50, 59, and 20 percentage points for the three task pairs. Evaluation uses 100 newly randomized layouts per task.
